Smart Automatic Wheelchair

A joystick-controlled mobility prototype that retrofitted a standard wheelchair with powered movement, ultrasonic obstacle alerts and GPS-based remote tracking.

Project brief

The project set out to automate a standard wheelchair through a practical mechatronics design. The team retrofitted a wheelchair frame with DC motors and a joystick control system, then added sensing and tracking features to support safer movement and remote location monitoring.

The report's design is joystick-controlled: the user chooses the direction of travel. Obstacle sensing and alerts support the user while manoeuvring; the project does not demonstrate autonomous route planning.

System architecture

The design brought together mechanical, electrical and software elements:

  • Joystick input: provides the user's directional commands.
  • Arduino UNO: coordinates the control circuit, sensors and actuators.
  • Motor drive: two 24V DC motors provide powered movement to the wheelchair.
  • Obstacle alert: an ultrasonic sensor detects nearby obstacles and a buzzer alerts the user. The report's results specifically describe this alert during reverse movement.
  • Temperature sensing: a DHT11 sensor is included for temperature monitoring.
  • Location monitoring: a GPS module and ESP32 connect the system to cloud services and a web app for remote tracking.

The report also documents a block diagram, electrical circuit diagrams and a 3D computer-aided design of the wheelchair.

Build & implementation

The team chose an existing wheelchair as the mechanical base rather than fabricating the entire frame from scratch. The frame was purchased in January 2022, after which fabrication, alterations and installation of the electrical components began.

The meeting log records the main build milestones: the software prototype was presented to the group on 27 January 2022; mechanical fabrication and axle retrofitting were completed by 3 February; and the GPS tracking software was completed on 8 February 2022.

Implementation work was organized across three divisions: mechanical, electrical and software. This structure brought the wheelchair frame, motor-control hardware, embedded control and web-based tracking into one project.

My role

I served as the Group Leader for the project at the Federal University of Technology, Minna. I organized the team into mechanical, electrical and software divisions, shared the group structure and assigned responsibilities. I also coordinated planning discussions around the project budget, component selection, power requirements and prototype design.

The project was a collaborative team effort; the report records contributions and implementation activities across the three divisions.

Results & constraints

The report states that the wheelchair could be directed with the joystick through the microcontroller. It also records an ultrasonic-sensor and buzzer alert when the chair approached an obstacle while moving backwards. A GPS tracker and web app were included to support remote location monitoring.

Component availability and cost were identified as development challenges. The report also notes that the prototype did not have an onboard battery and depended on a continuous external power source. Improved speed control and a rechargeable battery arrangement were identified as areas for further development.

Next iteration

  • Introduce an onboard rechargeable battery and a suitable charging arrangement.
  • Improve speed control so the user can increase or reduce movement speed as required.

These improvements were identified in the original project report as ways to improve the system's practicality and efficiency.

Project scope: This case study documents an academic team prototype. The report does not provide evidence of clinical evaluation, mobility-device certification or autonomous navigation validation.
